Three mechanisms · logical → physical architecture

What it costs to turn a WHAT into a HOW

Architecture states what a system must do. Design states how it will do it. Moving between those two layers looks like one smooth motion, and it is not: going up costs nothing, going down has to be bought, and what you buy it with is evidence. These three mechanisms describe the direction of that move, its price, and what the structure is made of at each end.

Mechanism 01 · direction

The Asymmetric Derivation

The two directions on the axis are not opposites of one another. Up is subtraction. Down is exclusion. Only one of them is free.

Both directions move, only one is cheap

You can abstract a built mechanism, and you can build from an abstract one. The difference shows up when you ask what each move costs — and the costs are nothing alike.

Going up is subtraction

To turn a mechanism into a general statement you delete the parts that were local to it: the material, the tolerance, the supplier. You don't have to learn anything to delete them, so the move is free and available immediately.

What you drop isn't stored anywhere. The general statement is a summary of work, not the work, which is why you can't run the move backwards later.

Going down has to be paid for

A design is a set of commitments, and each commitment rules out every other version the general statement would have allowed equally. Nothing inside the abstract layer can choose between them — it was built to be indifferent.

So the ruling-out has to come from outside: a load that breaks one option, a cost that kills another, a measurement that eliminates a third. Evidence is what you pay with.

The gap in between

One logical statement fits a whole family of real designs. That family is the room you still have to move in, and its size is the whole substance of design work.

Keeping the statement free of implementation keeps the family large, which is the only reason the trade-offs are still visible. Each commitment shrinks it — including options nobody has looked at yet.

Combining abstractions only widens them

Merge several high-level models without new information and all that survives is what they agree on. Everything they disagree about is dropped, because dropping is the only free move.

The merged statement therefore allows every design that any of the inputs allowed. It covers more and decides less. A stack of abstractions isn't a nearly-finished design; it's a broader permission.

Two ways this goes wrong

All the way up: models get generalized, merged and re-merged. Everything stays true, nothing gets decided, and no design is ever produced.

Straight down: the design is written in one stroke before anything has been tested, so every later mistake is found after the whole structure depends on it.

What follows

Stay general for as long as staying general is free. Spend that room only when something real has eliminated specific alternatives. When two groups disagree about a design, adding another layer of abstraction raises the permission and settles nothing — the disagreement is about which option to kill, and nothing upstream can kill it.

Takeaway

You can always compress many designs into one statement for free. You can never expand one statement into a design without paying, and you can never stack statements into a sharper one at all.

Mechanism 02 · price

The Funded Descent

Evidence is the currency. How far down you are when you spend it is the price. Order is the only part you control.

One purchase, two terms

Mechanism 01 says every step down has to be bought. This one adds the part that was missing: the same piece of evidence does not cost the same at every height. Together they say the descent isn't just slow — it has to be ordered.

How many commitments are already standing

Early on, a mistake touches a few things and can be cut out and replaced. Later, the same mistake breaks many commitments at once, because they have been locked to each other.

Specifying is the same act as raising that count. You cannot add detail without raising the price of being wrong.

You can't go back up to fix it

The instinct on finding a deep mistake is to return to the high-level model, correct it, and come back down. That doesn't work: the model is a summary, not the structure, so fixing it gives you a corrected sentence and not a corrected design.

The real route is to tear down what rests on the mistake and derive it again, which means re-buying every commitment above it — including the ones that happened to be right.

A staircase, not a slide

Each tread is a commitment and each riser is the evidence that paid for it. A new level of detail opens only after the one under it has survived contact with something real.

A level that has survived becomes the surface the next level is checked against, so settled work makes later mistakes easier to spot and cheaper to fix.

The ratio

Efficiency here is information gained ÷ options destroyed. Both are countable: how many alternatives you ruled out, and how many of those were ruled out by something you can name.

Destroy faster than you learn and the design is asserted. Learn without ever destroying and you never leave the free layer. Keeping them level is what earns the result.

Why the best design wasn't available at the top

Seeing which version is best depends on the same contact that funds the commitments. Before that, the differences that separate a good design from an adequate one aren't visible yet. The ideal design wasn't missed at the start — it didn't exist to be written.

Working rules

Settle every question at the earliest point where it still means something; cheap answers are the only cheap ones you get. Open a new level of detail only against something you can name. And treat a deferred decision as borrowing — it gets more expensive the later you take it, and the easy repair isn't available.

Takeaway

Because the way back up is closed, the only defence against an unaffordable mistake is buying each decision at the height where it is still cheap.

Mechanism 03 · interior

The Welded Interior

The first two describe the traffic between the layers. This one says what the layers are made of: tested connections over territory still open.

The measure

Not size, not completeness, not how many rules are written down. It is how many connections have been tested and hold weight, divided by how much ground the structure still covers.

Two documents of the same length can be worlds apart: one is a warehouse of items sitting near each other, the other is a crystal where every bond carries load.

Why the top layer is thin

A logical architecture holds a few relations spread over an enormous range of possible designs. Small top, huge bottom — by its own best quality it is the thinnest thing on the axis.

That is exactly why it can't settle an argument between two implementations, and exactly why it is still the right thing to hold: there is room left for evidence to act on.

Both halves move together

Each piece of evidence does two things in one stroke: it welds a connection into place, and it removes the options it just ruled out. Top goes up, bottom goes down.

So a finished design is not a more detailed version of the logical one. It is the same object with the ratio turned over.

Counting is not weighing

Writing a constraint into the model raises the cost of being wrong the moment it is written. It adds strength only if something outside the model established it.

An unproven constraint is a line on a drawing: it raises the price of error without raising the chance of being right.

The warehouse

A design written top to bottom in one go spends the room at full rate and welds nothing. It reviews well — every constraint stated, every interface named — and fails when something moves, because a connection that was never loaded has no known behaviour under load.

Linked, or just listed

Real systems don't open to requirements satisfied one at a time; several things have to hold at once. A model can name every variable and still miss the relation tying them together.

The test is to pick one component, change it, and ask what else is forced to move. If the answer is nothing, the constraints were listed, not linked.

What the density buys

Past a point the ratio stops being a score and starts acting like weight. On contact, the denser structure absorbs the thinner one's observations as special cases of what it has already worked out; the thin one cannot do the reverse.

Everything built underneath has to resolve against those tested connections. That, and not a position on an org chart, is what architectural authority actually is.

Working rules

Measure the ratio, not the page count. Prefer testing a constraint you already have over adding one you don't. Carry anything unproven as an assumption, because it is priced as structure and behaves as a hole. And don't close off ground you aren't ready to test — that's how a warehouse gets built.

Takeaway

The difference between a crystal and a warehouse never shows up in the constraint count, because counting records what you wrote and only evidence records what holds.

How the three fit

One move, read three ways

Each mechanism answers a different question about the same step down the axis.

01 · which way, and what with

Direction. Up is free and lossy; down has to be bought with evidence. Merging what you already hold never produces a design.

02 · how much, and in what order

Price. The same evidence is cheap early and expensive late, and the easy repair route is closed — so the order of steps is the whole defence.

03 · what the result is made of

Substance. Each purchase welds a connection and removes an option, so the structure grows denser as it descends — or hollow, if nothing was ever tested.

The whole thing in four moves

  1. Stay general while it's free. Nothing is lost by holding the WHAT open, and options closed early are closed before anyone could judge them.
  2. Buy each step. One named piece of evidence per level of detail. Anything closed by argument alone has only been postponed, and it will be re-priced later.
  3. Validate before you build on it. A level that hasn't met reality isn't a foundation, it's an assumption with the whole design resting on it.
  4. Check the ratio, not the volume. What fraction of what you've written has actually been tested, against how much ground you've already given up.
